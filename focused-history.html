The Focused History Across Chief Concerns

A complete history asks everything. A focused history asks what this concern demands, and the concern decides the questions. Pick what the patient came in with and read down. Cardinal features and red flags come first, because the dangerous questions belong early in the encounter, not tacked on at the end. Where an answer separates something dangerous from something benign, the right-hand column says so.

Scope. These banks are pediatric, drawn from the illness-script work behind the MedSimAI cases. Questions are phrased for a caregiver in most concerns and for the young person in others, so adjust the wording to whoever is answering. Twelve concerns are here now and more are being added.

Headache

Cardinal features and associated symptoms5

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • When did the headache start, and how has it changed since?
  • Where is the pain located?
  • How long does each episode last?
  • What does the pain feel like (pulsing, pressure, sharp)?
  • Are there associated symptoms (vomiting, fever, photophobia)?Fever points to meningitis, morning vomiting to raised pressure, photophobia to either meningitis or migraine.

AskWhy it matters

  • Is there neck stiffness or a rash?Meningitis. Ask early and act on it.
  • Did the headache start very suddenly like a thunderclap?Thunderclap onset is subarachnoid haemorrhage or AVM rupture until excluded.

Vomiting

with or without diarrhea31 questions
Cardinal features and associated symptoms15

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • When did the vomiting start?Acute is infectious, metabolic, toxic or surgical. Chronic is gastrointestinal. Cyclic is usually migraine, arrhythmia or UPJ obstruction.
  • Is it forceful or projectile?Projectile non-bilious vomiting at 2 to 8 weeks is pyloric stenosis.
  • What does the vomit look like—bilious, bloody, undigested food?Bilious emesis in a neonate is a surgical emergency until proven otherwise. Malrotation with midgut volvulus.
  • How often is the vomiting happening?
  • Is the child keeping anything down?
  • Is the child still hungry after vomiting, or refusing feeds?The hungry vomiter is pyloric stenosis; poor feeding suggests a metabolic or intracranial cause.
  • Has the child had any diarrhea or change in stool pattern?Vomiting with diarrhoea is usually gastroenteritis. Isolated vomiting is far wider: raised pressure, pneumonia, ingestion, diabetic ketoacidosis.
  • Any blood in the stool or complete absence of stool (obstipation)?Currant jelly stool is intussusception; no stool at all suggests obstruction.
  • Has the child lost weight or had trouble gaining weight?
  • Is there any timing pattern to the vomiting?Early-morning vomiting can mean raised intracranial pressure. Vomiting soon after eating suggests outlet obstruction or peptic ulcer.
  • Is the vomiting effortless?Effortless and painless is reflux. True vomiting is a noxious event with pain and retching.
  • Is there any blood in the vomitus?Haematemesis is darker, acidic and comes with retching. Haemoptysis is bright, frothy, alkaline and comes with respiratory symptoms.
  • Is undigested food present in the vomitus?Common in reflux. Old food suggests gastric outlet obstruction or a motility disorder.
  • Is there any abdominal pain?Right lower quadrant suggests appendicitis, right upper biliary, flank renal. Pain relieved by vomiting suggests ulcer; pancreatitis is not.
  • Is fever present?Points to gastroenteritis, appendicitis or an extraintestinal infection such as meningitis or a urinary tract infection.
Red flags6

Second on purpose. These are the answers that change your plan today, so ask them while you are still on the presenting problem rather than at the end.

AskWhy it matters
  • Is the child lethargic or unusually sleepy?Lethargy with vomiting means intracranial, metabolic, or shock.
  • Are there signs of dehydration (dry mouth, poor urine output)?Decides whether this child needs fluids now.
  • Is there a bulging fontanelle or unusual head size?Raised intracranial pressure in an infant.
  • Any confusion, deep breathing, fruity breath, or rapid breathing?Kussmaul breathing and fruity breath are DKA.
  • Does the child have persistent vomiting with poor growth or concern for abuse?Chronic vomiting with failure to thrive needs a cause found, not reassurance.
  • Is fecal material present in the emesis?Uncommon, and suggests distal obstruction: Hirschsprung disease, peritonitis, or a gastrocolic fistula.

Diarrhea

acute or persistent28 questions
Cardinal features and associated symptoms8

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • When did the diarrhea start?Under two weeks is acute and usually infectious. Chronic raises malabsorption, celiac disease and cystic fibrosis.
  • How frequent are the stools? Are they watery, bloody, or mucousy?Acutely, blood or mucus means an enteroinvasive organism. Chronically, inflammatory bowel disease. In an ill-appearing child, haemolytic uraemic syndrome.
  • Is the child also vomiting?Bilious vomiting in a neonate or infant means an anatomic cause: malrotation or an incarcerated hernia.
  • Is the child eating normally?
  • Is there abdominal pain or cramping? Tenesmus?Acute cramping fits gastroenteritis. Chronic cramping or tenesmus raises inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Is there a fever?Acutely, an enteroinvasive organism or a systemic illness. In a toxic-appearing child, sepsis.
  • Is the diarrhea worse with oral intake? Is it improved with fasting?Separates osmotic diarrhoea, which is most of them, from secretory diarrhoea, which can be occult oncologic disease.
  • Is there a rash?Petechial or purpuric suggests Henoch-Schonlein purpura, or sepsis in an ill child. Dermatitis herpetiformis suggests celiac disease.
Red flags6

Second on purpose. These are the answers that change your plan today, so ask them while you are still on the presenting problem rather than at the end.

AskWhy it matters
  • Is the stool bloody or unusually foul-smelling?Bloody stool is bacterial, IBD, or HUS; bulky and foul-smelling is malabsorption.
  • Has the child shown signs of dehydration (dry mouth, no tears, poor urine)?The finding that decides whether this child goes home.
  • Any signs of weight loss or poor growth?Chronic diarrhoea with failure to thrive raises cystic fibrosis, celiac disease and inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Any unusual infections or prolonged fevers?Raises immunodeficiency.
  • Any petechiae, pallor, or lethargy?HUS. Pallor and petechiae after bloody diarrhoea is the classic sequence.
  • Does the patient appear systemically ill?Raises sepsis, and with oliguria, haemolytic uraemic syndrome.

AskWhy it matters
  • Any recent travel or new foods?Travellers' diarrhoea from unfamiliar E. coli, and parasites such as Entamoeba and Giardia.

Cough

acute or chronic10 questions
Cardinal features and associated symptoms4

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • Did the cough begin with an upper respiratory tract infection?Viral URI is the commonest cause. Young children have six to eight a year, which can look like a chronic cough.
  • In young infants, is there any history of conjunctivitis in association with a cough and tachypnea?Conjunctivitis with pneumonitis in a young infant suggests Chlamydia trachomatis.
  • How is the cough related to time of day and to daily activities?After eating suggests aspiration or reflux; with cold air or exercise, reactive airway disease; at night, postnasal drip.
  • Does the cough resolve with sleep?A cough that vanishes in sleep, or appears only when an adult is present, suggests a psychogenic cough.

Rash

new or changing skin findings8 questions
Cardinal features and associated symptoms6

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • What was the progression of the rash over time and the duration of the rash?Viral exanthems progress predictably. Measles starts at the hairline and moves down, scarlet fever on the upper trunk.
  • What is the configuration of the rash?Linear or geometric suggests contact dermatitis, dermatomal suggests zoster, annular vesicles and bullae suggest linear IgA disease.
  • Where is the rash distributed on the body?Contact dermatitis must match where the agent touched. Scabies spares the face except in infants; atopic dermatitis favours the flexures.
  • What is the color of the rash?Pigment change is usually postinflammatory. Erythema suggests an active inflammatory or vascular process.
  • What other skin symptoms (such as pain or pruritis) are present?Cellulitis is painful. Contact dermatitis, often mistaken for it, is itchy.
  • Are there associated symptoms, including fever or joint pain?Time the fever against the rash. In roseola the fever comes first.

Limp

or refusal to walk8 questions
Cardinal features and associated symptoms6

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • How did the symptoms evolve? Was there a sudden or gradual onset?Gradual onset suggests inflammation or overuse. Abrupt refusal to walk suggests injury or a septic joint.
  • Are there specific associated symptoms?Fever with pain suggests septic arthritis; bone pain with malaise, leukaemia; diarrhoea and weight loss, inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Does the child report pain?Pain suggests injury, infection or inflammation. A limp without pain points to neuromuscular, metabolic, congenital or developmental causes.
  • Could the pain be referred?Hip pathology refers to the knee and medial thigh. An acute abdomen or testicular torsion can present as refusal to walk.
  • How would you characterize the limp?Whether the child bears weight, and whether the gait is antalgic, narrows it quickly.
  • Do symptoms vary with the time of day?Worse in the morning and easing through the day is the rheumatologic gelling pattern. Progressive through the day suggests weakness; tumour pain is constant.

Cardinal features and associated symptoms2

The symptom itself and what travels with it: when it started, what it looks like, how it has behaved since, and what else appeared alongside it.

AskWhy it matters
  • Is the swelling due to lymphadenitis or lymphadenopathy?Redness, warmth and tenderness mean active infection. Swelling without them means enlargement alone.
  • Is the swelling acute, subacute, or chronic?Bacterial infection is acute and progressive. EBV, cat-scratch and tuberculosis are subacute. Congenital lesions are chronic until they superinfect.

AskWhy it matters

AskWhy it matters

How to use these. Cardinal features and red flags together carry the presenting problem, so work through both before you move on. What follows reframes the differential rather than settling it: what the child already carries, what they have been exposed to, and what home looks like. A concern with nothing red is a finding worth stating out loud.

On the right-hand column. It appears only where the answer separates a dangerous cause from a benign one. Most questions have no note, which does not make them optional. It means the answer narrows the picture rather than deciding it.
